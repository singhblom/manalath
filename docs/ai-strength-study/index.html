How strong is the Manalath AI?

Summary

Key finding: the stronger the players, the more moving first decides the game
3 games in 4
won by the first player between two of the strongest bots, a first-move edge of about +223 Elo. Between weak bots the edge is close to zero.

The most important result is about the game rather than the bot. Moving first is worth next to nothing between weak players, but once both sides reliably search three plies it is worth 150 to 250 Elo, and it is still growing at the top of our ladder. A stronger engine, or strong human players, will most likely see an even larger edge. The main cause is the first stone every search tier plays, white in the centre, which is the strongest opening there is. Rated games between strong players will need a balancing rule, such as a fixed opening or a swap rule. The handicap study looks at which first move would make the game fair.

The rest is about the AI. We rated it at fifteen points along its difficulty scale, from uniformly random play to a 150,000-node search. Search keeps paying all the way up, unlike in our first run: every fourfold increase in the node budget is worth roughly 100 to 130 Elo, and the strongest tier rates 1615. Below that, not blundering is still the cheapest strength there is. Taking a win when one is on the board, never completing your own quart and never leaving the opponent an immediate quint are together worth 814 Elo over random play, with no lookahead at all.

The in-game slider runs from 800 to 1600: from the quiet tier, which never throws a game away but otherwise plays at random, to just short of the 150,000-node search.

The ladder

Difficulty is a number from 0 to 7 in packages/shared/src/ai.js. Each whole number is a tier:

  1. Random. Any legal move, uniformly. This is the anchor, fixed at 0 Elo.
  2. Safe. Takes an immediate win if there is one, otherwise plays randomly among moves that don't lose on the spot.
  3. Quiet. Safe, and also avoids moves that leave the opponent an immediate win.
  4. 1-ply search with the hand-written evaluation.
  5. Search, 3,000 nodes, with iterative deepening.
  6. Search, 12,000 nodes.
  7. Search, 50,000 nodes.
  8. Search, 150,000 nodes. Added once the engine got fast enough to afford it.

A fractional difficulty mixes the two neighbouring tiers on each move. At 3.4, the bot plays as tier 4 on 40% of its moves and as tier 3 on the rest. Search is budgeted in nodes rather than milliseconds, so a given difficulty is equally strong on a fast desktop and a slow phone. The negamax explainer describes the search itself.

Method

scripts/rate-ai.js plays bots at every half step from 0 to 7 against their neighbours one half step and one full step away, with colours alternating. The current ratings pool 10,000 games from two independent runs, 400 per pairing up to difficulty 6 and 200 for pairings with 6.5 or 7, averaging 19.7 moves each. A game is only counted if it was played by the current engine (version 3) and both of its bots would still play the same tiers today, so games from before the 150,000-node tier was added still count wherever they are unaffected.

All games are fitted at once by maximum likelihood with a Bradley–Terry model, the model behind Elo. The model gives each difficulty its own first-move advantage as well as its own rating; a game's advantage is the mean of its two players' terms. That turned out to matter: a single shared term, as in the first run, is a poor fit once the stronger bots are included (see Moving first). Fitting everything at once means the result doesn't depend on the order the games were played and comes with standard errors.

Four things stand out.

  • The big steps are still cheap. Random to safe is worth about 420 Elo, and safe to quiet another 400. Neither needs any search. The most lopsided pairing in the run was difficulty 2.5 against 1.5, which won 382 of 400.
  • Mixing tiers isn't linear. Half-random, half-safe play (difficulty 0.5) already rates 297, most of the way to fully safe at 419. One suicidal move ends a game, so avoiding even half of them gains most of the benefit. The slider maps Elo to difficulty through the measured half steps, so it absorbs this.
  • 1-ply search is a small step, deeper search a big one. The 1-ply tier gains only 160 over quiet play. The first real search budget, 3,000 nodes, gains another 290.
  • More search keeps paying. From 3,000 to 12,000 nodes is worth +98, from 12,000 to 50,000 +133, and the threefold step to 150,000 another +118. Each search tier from 5 to 7 now beats the one below it 62 to 70% of the time, where in the first run tiers 4 to 6 were indistinguishable.

What search buys

Each search tier deepens iteratively until its node budget runs out, and plays the move from the deepest iteration it finished. Budgets are fixed, so the depth reached depends on the position. The engine now searches about 220 nodes per millisecond, so even the top tier answers in well under a second.

The pattern fits the ratings. Each tier's gain comes from moving a larger share of positions one ply deeper: 3,000 nodes is mostly a depth-2 player, 12,000 finishes depth 3 about a third of the time, 50,000 mostly finishes depth 3, and 150,000 finishes depth 4 or more in almost half of positions. Going much further in the browser would take a smarter search rather than a bigger budget; the negamax explainer lists the options.

Moving first

In the first run, with all search tiers stuck at depth 2, the first-move advantage came out at 9 ± 12 Elo, which is nothing. With deeper search it is large. The model's per-difficulty terms stay within about 50 Elo of zero up to difficulty 5, then rise to +156 at difficulty 6 and +223 at difficulty 7. Between two difficulty-7 bots, +223 means the first player wins about 78% of games.

The raw pairings tell the same story without the model. Every pairing between two bots of difficulty 5.5 or more gave the first player at least 66%, and the two pairings involving difficulty 7 gave 75% and 77%. The direct measurement between two difficulty-6 bots, 82 of 100 games (about +260 Elo), is higher than the fitted +156 ± 20. The fitted term for a difficulty is estimated from games against its neighbours and smoothed towards them, so it is best read as a trend. The direct figure is the better number for two equal bots.

The advantage is mostly about the opening. All of the search tiers open with a white stone in the centre, and the opening study found that this is the strongest first move there is. It also found that a white first stone on c3, one of the star points next to the centre, leaves two difficulty-6 or difficulty-7 bots close to even. That makes a fixed c3 opening a natural rule for games between equals.

For the ratings themselves, the per-difficulty first-move term is what keeps the fit honest. Games at the top are played with colours alternating, so ratings are colour-averaged, but a single shared term would be pulled between near-zero at the bottom and 200 at the top and bend every rating around it.

What changed since the first run

The first run, with 920 games, found a plateau. Tiers 4 to 6 rated 1048, 1092 and 1061, the same player within noise, and we concluded that search stopped paying at about depth 2. Two bugs made that look true when it wasn't.

  • Engine 2: exact ties. The root search counted moves that had merely been cut off as ties for best and picked among them at random. Once a forced win was found, the bot played it only about four times in ten. Deeper searches find more forced wins, so they threw away more of them. Fixing it lifted tiers 4 to 6 to 1104, 1221 and 1288 in a re-rating of another 920 games.
  • Engine 3: speed. Engine 2 managed about 6 nodes per millisecond, so the 50,000-node tier took six seconds a move and was really limited by the 4-second safety cap. Labelling the groups once per node made the search 35 times faster with identical decisions. Every tier now gets its full budget, which made room for the 150,000-node tier.

Both are described in the negamax explainer. The tiers without search rated the same within noise in every run, as they should: quiet play came out at 756 in the first run and 814 now.

The slider

The slider runs from 800 to 1600 Elo in steps of 25 and is converted to a difficulty by linear interpolation over the measured points in packages/shared/src/ai-ratings.json. The bottom end is the quiet tier. The top end, 1600, sits just below the 150,000-node tier's 1615, and plays as that tier on about three moves in four and as the 50,000-node tier on the rest. Settings below 800 would mean a bot that sometimes loses on the spot, which is too easy to be interesting.

The top of the scale is now set by what a browser can afford per move rather than by the engine hitting a wall. Running bun run rate-ai adds games to scripts/ai-games.jsonl and refits, keeping every game that still applies. When the search changes how the bots play, ENGINE in ai.js is bumped and older games drop out.

Limitations

  • The absolute scale is only as good as its lowest links. Every rating is chained up from random play, so noise in the easiest pairings shifts every rating above them. Two independent runs of about 5,000 games disagreed by up to 160 Elo at the top, mostly because random play against difficulty 0.5 came out differently. Pooling both halves that risk. Gaps between neighbouring difficulties are much better determined than the absolute values.
  • The top of the ladder has the fewest games. 200 games per pairing involving difficulty 6.5 or 7, so the standard error at difficulty 7 is ±43 against the anchor and ±33 on its first-move term.
  • These are self-play ratings. They measure how the bots do against each other, not against people. A human rated 1000 on some future ladder won't necessarily win half their games against the 1000 bot, until the bots take part in rated human games and anchor that scale too.
  • Colour matters at the top. The ratings are colour-averaged. Against a difficulty-7 bot, which colour you play is worth more than the whole step from difficulty 6 to 7.
